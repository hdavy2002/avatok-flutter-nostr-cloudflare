# Symbols, mandalas and yantras

### symbol-om
- topic: symbol
- title: Om (Aum), the sacred syllable
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity:
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

Om (also written Aum) is the sacred syllable of Hindu tradition. The Mandukya Upanishad explains it as made of the sounds A, U and M, plus the silence after them, and devotees read these as waking, dreaming and deep sleep, with silence as the fourth state beyond them. Om begins most mantras and prayers and is written in Devanagari as a shape like a numeral 3 with a curl, a crescent and a dot. It is linked to no single deity or weekday and is used by Hindus, Buddhists and Jains in their own ways.

### symbol-om-usage
- topic: symbol
- title: How Om is used in worship and design
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity:
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

Om is chanted at the start of prayer, yoga and meditation, drawn at the entrance of homes and temples, and worn as a pendant. Because it is regarded as sacred, many devotees prefer that Om appears with respect, for example not on footwear or places considered unclean. A designer or wearer may choose an Om with a lotus, a sun, or a mandala. It is suitable on any day, though some devotees link it with Sunday and the Sun as the source of light.

### symbol-swastika
- topic: symbol
- title: The swastika in Hinduism, an auspicious symbol
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: ganesha
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The swastika (from Sanskrit "svasti", well-being) is an ancient auspicious symbol in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism, used for thousands of years in India. Hindus draw it, often in red kumkum, at doorways, on new account books at Diwali, at weddings and at Griha Pravesh. Its four arms are read as the four directions, four Vedas or four aims of life (purusharthas). It is closely associated with Ganesha and Lakshmi. It is distinct from the tilted emblem adopted by the Nazi party in the twentieth century, so context should always be explained to avoid misunderstanding.

### symbol-trishul
- topic: symbol
- title: Trishul, Shiva's trident
- graha:
- weekday: monday
- deity: shiva
- source: Shiva Purana
- lang: en

The trishul is the three-pronged trident carried by Shiva and also by Durga in many images. Devotees read the three prongs in different ways, such as creation, preservation and destruction, or past, present and future, or the three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas). The damru is often tied to it. It is linked to Monday, Mahashivaratri and Shiva worship in general. Interpretations differ between schools, and the symbol is popular in devotional art and on T-shirts for devotees of Shiva.

### symbol-damru
- topic: symbol
- title: Damru, Shiva's drum
- graha:
- weekday: monday
- deity: shiva
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The damru is the small hourglass-shaped drum held by Shiva, especially in his Nataraja form. Tradition links its sound with the rhythm of creation and with the origin of Sanskrit, since the Maheshwara Sutras, a list of the Sanskrit sound groups, are traditionally said to have come from its beats. The drum is shown in Shiva's hand along with the trishul. It is associated with Monday and Shiva worship and is a popular motif in temple art and devotional designs.

### symbol-shankh
- topic: symbol
- title: Shankh, the conch
- graha:
- weekday: thursday
- deity: vishnu
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The shankh (conch shell) is carried by Vishnu, and Krishna's conch Panchajanya is blown in the Mahabharata. Blowing a conch begins many Hindu ceremonies, aartis and temple rituals, and its sound is considered auspicious. In the Mahabharata, conches announce the start of the Kurukshetra war. The shankh is also kept in many home shrines and is linked with Lakshmi, who rose from the ocean as the conch did, in popular tradition. It relates to Vishnu worship and Thursday.

### symbol-sudarshan-chakra
- topic: symbol
- title: Sudarshan Chakra, Vishnu's discus
- graha:
- weekday: thursday
- deity: vishnu
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The Sudarshan Chakra is the spinning discus with serrated edges that Vishnu holds in one hand. Devotees read it as the wheel of time and of dharma, a symbol of protection and of righteous power. Krishna is also shown with the Sudarshan Chakra in many stories. It is a major motif in Vaishnava art and the emblem of the Sri Vaishnava tradition, shown on flags and temple pillars. It is linked to Vishnu worship and Thursday, and to Ekadashi.

### symbol-lotus
- topic: symbol
- title: Padma, the lotus
- graha:
- weekday: friday
- deity: lakshmi
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The lotus (padma) grows from mud yet rises clean, so it is read as purity, spiritual unfolding and detachment. Lakshmi sits on a lotus, Saraswati sits on a white lotus, Vishnu holds a lotus, and Brahma is born from one at Vishnu's navel. The lotus is also the basis of chakra imagery. It is linked with Friday and Lakshmi worship, and with the colours white and pink. The lotus is India's national flower and a common motif in Hindu art and designs.

### symbol-kalash
- topic: symbol
- title: Kalash, the sacred pot
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: lakshmi
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The kalash is a pot filled with water, topped with mango leaves and a coconut, and is placed at the start of many pujas, weddings and Griha Pravesh ceremonies. It is read as a symbol of abundance and as the seat of the deities invoked, and is linked to the churning of the ocean and the pot of nectar. It is closely associated with Lakshmi and Varuna, and appears in Navratri Ghatasthapana. Decorations on it, such as kumkum, a swastika and a red thread, vary by region.

### symbol-sri-yantra
- topic: symbol
- title: Sri Yantra, structure and meaning
- graha:
- weekday: friday
- deity: lakshmi
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The Sri Yantra, also called Sri Chakra, is a diagram of nine interlocking triangles, four pointing upward and five pointing downward, forming forty-three smaller triangles, surrounded by two rings of lotus petals and a square gateway (bhupura). Tradition links the upward triangles with Shiva and the downward ones with Shakti, meeting at the central point (bindu). It is the principal yantra of Sri Vidya, the Tripura Sundari tradition, and in popular practice is also associated with Lakshmi, prosperity and Friday. Its use in worship is guided by lineage teachers.

### symbol-sri-yantra-use
- topic: symbol
- title: Sri Yantra in homes and on art
- graha:
- weekday: friday
- deity: lakshmi
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Many homes and shops keep a Sri Yantra, often in metal, as a sacred diagram, and some devotees worship it on Fridays or during Navratri and Diwali. Customs for worship differ, and traditional Sri Vidya sources say the formal practice should be learned from a teacher. As a design on a T-shirt it is a geometric symbol of the Goddess, and the wearer can enjoy it without any promise of results.

### symbol-navagraha-yantra
- topic: symbol
- title: Navagraha Yantra or mandala
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity:
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

A Navagraha yantra or mandala is a diagram that places the nine grahas together, often in a three-by-three grid with Surya in the centre, or in a circular arrangement with each graha's colour. Temples with Navagraha shrines use a similar layout. Devotees worship it to honour all nine grahas at once, for example during Navagraha puja or a graha shanti. The colours follow the commonly given scheme for each graha, which differs by region. It is a devotional diagram, and lineage teachers may give differing layouts.

### symbol-hanuman-yantra
- topic: symbol
- title: Hanuman Yantra
- graha: mangal
- weekday: tuesday
- deity: hanuman
- source: Regional practice (varies)
- lang: en

The Hanuman Yantra is a geometric diagram, often a hexagram or square with bija syllables and the name of Hanuman, worshipped in some traditions along with the Hanuman Chalisa. Devotees keep it at home or in a shop, and it is usually associated with Tuesday and Saturday and with the colours red and orange. Its exact design differs between texts and lineages, so no single form is standard. As a design on a T-shirt it is a symbol of Hanuman's strength and devotion.

### symbol-ganesha-yantra
- topic: symbol
- title: Ganesha Yantra
- graha:
- weekday: wednesday
- deity: ganesha
- source: Regional practice (varies)
- lang: en

The Ganesha Yantra is a geometric diagram, usually triangle- or lotus-based, with the seed syllable "Gam" and the name of Ganesha. Devotees worship it in some traditions at the start of new ventures, on Wednesdays and on Ganesh Chaturthi. Its exact form varies between texts and lineages. As a design it combines sacred geometry with the beloved elephant-headed deity, and is read as a symbol of auspicious beginnings and obstacles being removed, as tradition describes.

### symbol-yantra-general
- topic: symbol
- title: What a yantra is
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity:
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

A yantra is a geometric diagram used in Tantric and devotional practice as a seat for a deity or as an object of meditation. It is typically made of triangles, circles, lotus petals and a square frame, with a central point (bindu), and sometimes with seed syllables. Different deities have different yantras. Traditional use includes consecration (prana pratishtha) and mantra recitation under guidance. A yantra on a T-shirt is a design inspired by sacred geometry and does not substitute for such practice.

### symbol-mandala-general
- topic: symbol
- title: Mandala in Hindu tradition
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity:
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

A mandala (Sanskrit for circle) is a circular, symmetrical diagram that represents the cosmos or the seat of a deity, with a central point from which the design radiates outward. Mandalas appear in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions, with different meanings in each. In Hindu worship, temple floors, rangoli and diagrams drawn for pujas use mandala forms, and the Navagraha mandala and Sri Yantra are examples. In design, a mandala is often used as a calming, meditative motif with lotus petals or deity symbols at the centre.

### symbol-gada
- topic: symbol
- title: Gada, the mace
- graha: mangal
- weekday: tuesday
- deity: hanuman
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The gada (mace) is a heavy club carried by Hanuman, by Vishnu (as Kaumodaki) and by Bhima in the Mahabharata. It is read by devotees as strength used in service of dharma. In Hanuman imagery it is a defining feature, along with red or saffron colour. It is related to Tuesday and Saturday worship of Hanuman and to wrestlers' akhadas, where Hanuman is the patron. It is a popular motif in designs of Hanuman and Vishnu.

### symbol-rudraksha
- topic: symbol
- title: Rudraksha beads
- graha:
- weekday: monday
- deity: shiva
- source: Shiva Purana
- lang: en

Rudraksha are the seeds of a tree, worn or used as a mala (prayer-bead string) by devotees of Shiva. The name means "eye of Rudra", and tradition, including the Shiva Purana, tells of beads born from Shiva's tears. Beads are classed by their natural lines, called mukhi, with the five-faced bead most common, and traditional texts attribute different meanings to each. A 108-bead rudraksha mala is used to count mantras such as "Om Namah Shivaya". Beliefs about each type vary, and the beads are devotional items.

### symbol-diya
- topic: symbol
- title: Diya, the oil lamp
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: lakshmi
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The diya is an oil or ghee lamp lit in nearly every Hindu puja. It is read as the light of knowledge removing darkness, which is the meaning behind Diwali, the festival of lights. Ghee lamps are commonly offered to Vishnu and Lakshmi, and sesame or mustard oil lamps to Shani in many customs. Lighting a lamp at dawn or dusk in front of the home shrine is a widely followed daily practice. Its form varies from clay diyas to large brass temple lamps.

### symbol-peacock-feather
- topic: symbol
- title: Peacock feather (mor pankh)
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: krishna
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

The peacock feather is a signature element of Krishna's crown and is used in devotional art, home shrines and decoration. It is also linked with Kartikeya, whose vahana is the peacock, and with Saraswati in some depictions. Devotees read the many colours of the feather as the richness of creation. The peacock is India's national bird. The feather is a popular T-shirt motif for devotees of Krishna, and has no fixed weekday.
