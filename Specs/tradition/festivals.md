# Festivals

### festival-maha-shivaratri
- topic: festival
- title: Maha Shivaratri
- graha: chandra
- weekday: monday
- deity: shiva
- source: Shiva Purana
- lang: en

Maha Shivaratri, the "great night of Shiva", falls on the fourteenth lunar night of the dark half of the month of Phalguna (Magha by the North Indian purnimanta count), usually in February or March. Devotees fast, keep a night-long vigil (jagran), bathe the Shivling with milk, water and honey, offer bael leaves and chant "Om Namah Shivaya". Tradition describes it as the night of Shiva's marriage to Parvati in some regions, and as the night of the Lingodbhava, the pillar of light, in others. White is commonly worn, with no strict rule.

### festival-shravan-somvar
- topic: festival
- title: Shravan Somvar, the Mondays of Shravan
- graha: chandra
- weekday: monday
- deity: shiva
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Shravan (also called Sawan) is a monsoon month, around July and August, that is especially dear to Shiva, and its Mondays (Shravan Somvar) are observed with fasts and Shivling abhishek. In North India many pilgrims make the Kanwar Yatra, carrying Ganga water to Shiva temples. White is a common colour, and green, linked to the rains, is also widely worn in the month in North India. The dates of the month differ between the North (purnimanta) and South or West (amanta) calendars, so the exact weeks vary by region.

### festival-navratri-overview
- topic: festival
- title: Navratri, the two main Navratris
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: durga
- source: Devi Mahatmya (Durga Saptashati)
- lang: en

Navratri means "nine nights" and is the festival of the Goddess. Two are widely celebrated: Chaitra Navratri in March or April, which ends with Ram Navami, and Sharad Navratri in September or October, the best known, ending with Vijayadashami (Dussehra). Two further Gupt (hidden) Navratris occur in other months. Devotees fast, worship Durga in nine forms, and read the Durga Saptashati. Garba and dandiya are performed in Gujarat, Durga Puja in Bengal, and doll displays called Golu in parts of South India.

### festival-chaitra-navratri
- topic: festival
- title: Chaitra Navratri
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: durga
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Chaitra Navratri begins on the first day of the Hindu lunar year in the month of Chaitra, which is the new year in many North and Central Indian calendars (celebrated as Ugadi and Gudi Padwa in the Deccan). Families set up a kalash (Ghatasthapana), sow barley seeds, fast, and worship the nine forms of the Goddess. The ninth day is Ram Navami, the birth of Rama. In many families, the colour red or orange is worn. Customs vary between regions.

### festival-sharad-navratri
- topic: festival
- title: Sharad Navratri and Vijayadashami
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: durga
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Sharad Navratri, in the month of Ashwin, is the principal nine-night festival of the Goddess. In Gujarat it is marked by garba and dandiya, in Bengal by Durga Puja from the sixth to tenth day, and in many northern towns by Ramlila performances that end with Dussehra, when effigies of Ravana are burnt. In the South, Saraswati Puja and Ayudha Puja fall on the last days. Many devotees fast for nine days and wear the day's colour. Practices vary widely.

### festival-navratri-nine-forms
- topic: festival
- title: The nine forms of Durga worshipped in Navratri
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: durga
- source: Devi Mahatmya (Durga Saptashati)
- lang: en

During Navratri, one form of the Goddess is worshipped each day in a common North Indian scheme: Shailaputri, Brahmacharini, Chandraghanta, Kushmanda, Skandamata, Katyayani, Kalaratri, Mahagauri and Siddhidatri. Shailaputri is the daughter of the mountain, Brahmacharini the ascetic, Skandamata the mother of Kartikeya, and Mahagauri the radiant white form. The names are given in a verse traditionally recited in the Devi Kavacha. Some regions follow other sequences, so order and emphasis vary.

### festival-navratri-day-colours
- topic: colour
- title: Navratri day colours, a modern popular custom
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: durga
- source: Modern convention
- lang: en

Wearing a different colour on each of the nine nights of Navratri is a modern popular custom, widely published in newspapers and on social media, and especially followed in Maharashtra, Gujarat and online. The colour usually follows the weekday on which each night falls, for example white on Monday, red on Tuesday, blue on Wednesday, yellow on Thursday, green on Friday, grey on Saturday and orange on Sunday. Lists differ between sources and years, and are not found in the classical texts. Many families simply wear red or any bright colour.

### festival-diwali-five-days
- topic: festival
- title: Diwali, the five days
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: lakshmi
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

Diwali, the festival of lights, is celebrated over five days around October or November: Dhanteras (worship of Dhanvantari and Lakshmi, with metal purchases), Naraka Chaturdashi, Lakshmi Puja on the new moon night, Govardhan Puja and Annakut, and Bhai Dooj. The reason for celebrating varies: return of Rama to Ayodhya in much of North India, Krishna's victory over Narakasura in the South, and Lakshmi's worship in many homes. Lamps (diyas), rangoli and new clothes are customary, with red, gold and yellow common.

### festival-diwali-lakshmi-puja
- topic: festival
- title: Diwali Lakshmi Puja
- graha: shukra
- weekday: friday
- deity: lakshmi
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

On the new moon night of Diwali, many homes worship Lakshmi together with Ganesha during the evening Pradosh period, using lamps, flowers, sweets and coins, and keep the entrance lit. Business families perform Chopda Pujan on new account books, and Kuber is also invoked. The timing is set each year by the panchang. In Bengal and Odisha the same night is Kali Puja. The colour most worn is red or gold, and customs vary by region.

### festival-ganesh-chaturthi
- topic: festival
- title: Ganesh Chaturthi
- graha:
- weekday: wednesday
- deity: ganesha
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Ganesh Chaturthi celebrates the birth of Ganesha on the fourth day of the bright half of Bhadrapada, around August or September. Clay idols are installed in homes and community pandals for one and a half, three, five, seven or ten days and immersed on Anant Chaturdashi. It is especially grand in Maharashtra, where Bal Gangadhar Tilak helped make it a public festival in the 1890s, and in Goa, Karnataka and Andhra. Modak is the favourite offering. Red, yellow and orange are commonly worn.

### festival-hanuman-jayanti
- topic: festival
- title: Hanuman Jayanti
- graha: mangal
- weekday: tuesday
- deity: hanuman
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Hanuman Jayanti marks the birth of Hanuman and is observed on Chaitra Purnima in most of North India, around March or April. Devotees visit temples before dawn, offer sindoor, red flowers and boondi, and recite the Hanuman Chalisa or Sundarkand. Other regions celebrate on different dates, for example in Tamil Nadu in the month of Margazhi, and in Andhra and Telangana at other times of the year. Red, orange and saffron are commonly worn. Dates vary by tradition.

### festival-janmashtami
- topic: festival
- title: Krishna Janmashtami
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: krishna
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

Janmashtami marks the birth of Krishna on the eighth day of the dark half of Bhadrapada (Shravana by the amanta calendar), around August or September, with the birth celebrated at midnight. Devotees fast until midnight, decorate cradles and tableaux (jhankis), sing bhajans and share butter and sweets. In Maharashtra, human pyramids break hanging pots in Dahi Handi. Yellow, blue and peacock shades are commonly worn. Vaishnava and Smarta traditions sometimes celebrate on different days, so dates differ.

### festival-ram-navami
- topic: festival
- title: Ram Navami
- graha: surya
- weekday:
- deity: rama
- source: Valmiki Ramayana
- lang: en

Ram Navami celebrates the birth of Rama on the ninth day of the bright half of Chaitra, around March or April, which is also the last day of Chaitra Navratri. Tradition places the birth at midday, and temples perform special aartis then. Devotees fast, read the Ramayana or Ramcharitmanas, and in Ayodhya large fairs are held. Panakam and kosambari are offered in the South. Saffron, yellow and light blue are commonly seen. Customs vary across regions.

### festival-makar-sankranti
- topic: festival
- title: Makar Sankranti
- graha: surya
- weekday: sunday
- deity: surya
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

Makar Sankranti marks the day the Sun enters Makara (Capricorn) around 14 or 15 January, and the start of Uttarayan, the Sun's northward journey. It is celebrated as Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Maghi in Punjab, the day after Lohri, Magh Bihu in Assam and with kite flying in Gujarat. Devotees take holy dips, honour Surya and share til-gud (sesame and jaggery). In Maharashtra black clothes are customarily worn on this day, and yellow, orange and red are common elsewhere.

### festival-vasant-panchami
- topic: festival
- title: Vasant Panchami
- graha: budh
- weekday:
- deity: saraswati
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Vasant Panchami falls on the fifth day of the bright half of Magha, around late January or February, and marks the arrival of spring. It is the main festival of Saraswati, whose worship includes placing books, pens and instruments before her image. Yellow is the traditional colour, in clothes and in food such as sweet yellow rice, linked with blooming mustard fields. Many families do Vidyarambh, the child's first writing, on this day. Kite flying is popular in Punjab and Haryana.

### festival-karva-chauth
- topic: festival
- title: Karva Chauth
- graha: chandra
- weekday:
- deity: parvati
- source: Common North Indian practice
- lang: en

Karva Chauth is observed on the fourth day after the full moon in the month of Kartik, mainly in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh. Married women fast from sunrise until moonrise, traditionally for the wellbeing and long life of their husbands, and then break the fast after seeing the moon through a sieve. The day includes worship of Shiva, Parvati and Ganesha and a Karva Chauth katha. Red, maroon and bridal colours are customary. Some husbands and unmarried women also fast now.

### festival-chhath
- topic: festival
- title: Chhath Puja
- graha: surya
- weekday:
- deity: surya
- source: Regional practice (varies)
- lang: en

Chhath is a four-day festival of Surya and Chhathi Maiya, observed on the sixth day of the bright half of Kartik, about six days after Diwali, mainly in Bihar, Jharkhand, eastern Uttar Pradesh and Nepal. Devotees fast for about thirty-six hours without water, stand in a river or pond and offer arghya to the setting Sun and to the rising Sun the next morning. Thekua and fruits are offered in bamboo baskets. Women often wear orange sindoor and bright saris. The festival is a distinctive regional tradition.

### festival-dussehra
- topic: festival
- title: Dussehra (Vijayadashami)
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: rama
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

Dussehra, also called Vijayadashami, falls on the tenth day after the start of Sharad Navratri. In North India it commemorates Rama's victory over Ravana, marked by Ramlila and burning effigies of Ravana. In Bengal and the East it marks the end of Durga Puja, with idol immersion. In Karnataka, Mysuru's Dasara is a royal tradition, and in many places tools, vehicles and books are worshipped (Ayudha Puja). It is considered an auspicious day for starting learning or new ventures in many families. Customs vary.

### festival-holi
- topic: festival
- title: Holi
- graha:
- weekday:
- deity: vishnu
- source: Widely followed tradition
- lang: en

Holi, the festival of colours, falls on the full moon of Phalguna (February or March). On the evening before, Holika Dahan, bonfires recall the story of Prahlad, who was devoted to Vishnu and survived the fire that burned his aunt Holika. The next day people play with coloured powders, and in Mathura and Vrindavan it is also linked to Krishna and Radha. White clothing is worn to show the colours. Regional versions include Lathmar Holi, Dol Jatra and Shigmo.
